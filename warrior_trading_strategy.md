
Warrior Trading Premarket News-Catalyst Strategy
Executive summary

The public version of the premarket strategy is a small-cap momentum system built around news-catalyst gappers that look “obvious” before the bell and continue through the open. Across official materials from Ross Cameron, the repeatable core is: scan for premarket gappers, verify a real catalyst, prefer low float plus unusually high relative volume, narrow to the top 2–3 obvious leaders, mark premarket highs / flags / pivots, and then trade breaks of premarket highs, premarket flag breaks, red-to-green moves, and 1-minute opening-range breakouts with very tight, predefined risk. Public risk rules repeatedly emphasize a 2:1 minimum reward/risk framework, fast partials, and immediate exits if momentum fails.

The most precise public “stock selection” rule-set now appears in Warrior’s official PDF , which formalizes Ross’s 5 pillars as: relative volume of at least 5x, stock already up at least 10% on the day, a visible catalyst, preferred price range of $1–$20, and low float / low supply. That PDF also says he typically focuses on the top 2–3 leading percentage gainers because the most obvious stocks tend to produce the best follow-through. This is tighter than the older public “Gap and Go” article, which starts from a broader rule of gaps over 4% and then narrows from there.

Operationally, Warrior’s public material assumes a trader with premarket market access, limit-order capability, real-time scanners, a news feed, Level 2, and enough execution speed to manage manual exits. His own materials say premarket trading is often done via scanners beginning well before the open; the support docs say Warrior scanners can be viewed from 4:00 a.m. ET, Top Gappers refresh every 30 seconds until 9:30 a.m. ET, and “Running Up” alerts are useful during the 7 a.m., 8 a.m., and 9 a.m. ET news-release windows. Recent Warrior material also says Ross’s most active trading window is roughly 7:00 a.m. to 11:00 a.m. ET, while older public video says he generally starts around 8–9 a.m. ET and trades actively through 10–11 a.m. ET.

A fair bottom line: the public Warrior method is not “buy any stock with news in premarket.” It is a narrow, momentum-chasing, crowd-participation strategy for the most obvious low-float / high-RVOL names, with success depending heavily on timing, liquidity, slippage control, and willingness to bail instantly when a breakout does not resolve. The biggest strengths are clarity and selectivity; the biggest weaknesses are fast reversals, widened spreads, hidden sellers, halts, extended names that have already made the move premarket, and the fact that public examples are not a random sample of all trades.

The public filters that matter most are therefore: obvious stock, obvious catalyst, obvious crowd attention. Warrior’s newer public PDF is especially useful because it sharpens the setup from a generic “gapper” into a stronger shortlist: 5x RVOL, +10% move, catalyst, $1–$20 price, and low float. The support article adds an important caveat: even Warrior’s own “5 Pillar” scanner still requires a manual news check via the flame indicator, because the scanner itself does not fully filter for valid news.

A few operational details are easy to miss but matter a lot. Warrior’s scanners can be viewed from 4:00 a.m. ET; Top Gappers refresh every 30 seconds and stop updating at 9:30 a.m. ET; alert scanners refresh every second; the “Running Up” scanner is explicitly recommended around the 7/8/9 a.m. ET news windows; and the news “flame” tells you whether a headline is 0–2 hours old, 2–12 hours old, or 12–24 hours old. That makes the public Warrior workflow less discretionary than it first appears: it is actually a scanner-led process with a manual catalyst check and then chart / tape confirmation.

The setup logic is also very consistent. In the older official Gap-and-Go page, the public checklist is: scan all gappers over 4%, hunt for catalyst, mark premarket highs and flag highs, prepare orders to buy those highs when the market opens, and at 9:30 buy either the premarket highs or the high of the first 1-minute candle with a stop at the low of that candle. The public setup list on that page names break of premarket flags, opening-range breakouts, and red-to-green moves as the main public entries. The course preview reinforces this by listing public chapter titles such as Break of Pre-Market Highs, Break of Pre-Market Pivot, 1-Minute / 5-Minute ORB, Red to Green, and Pre-Market Gap & Go Trades.

On risk, Warrior’s public material is surprisingly explicit. The momentum strategy page says Ross usually puts the stop just below the first pullback; if the stop would be wider than 20 cents, he may choose to cap the loss and re-enter later; and the trade should still support a 2:1 profit/loss ratio. The same page gives the sizing formula directly, explains that he sells half at the first target, then slides the stop to breakeven on the balance, and uses the first red candle or an extension bar as exit signals depending on whether partials were already taken. The newer trade-analysis PDF adds a specifically important premarket rule: when he trades premarket he cannot use stop orders, so his hand must be on the sell hotkey — “Breakout or Bailout.”

Finally, the public material shows that Warrior’s tooling has evolved. Older public pages mention outside tools like Trade Ideas and eSignal for float / scanning references, but the newer official screener article says Ross now considers Day Trade Dash his primary momentum scanner and says he no longer relies on third-party platforms in the same way. At the same time, the same newer article still recommends Thinkorswim, Webull, and TradingView as budget-capable alternatives, which means the strategy is portable even if Warrior now markets a first-party stack.

Comparative context

Warrior’s public strategy is closest to the broader “stocks in play at the open” school of day trading, but its public emphasis is notably more low-float, small-cap, gapper-centric than some peers. The strongest point of agreement among all four is that premarket prep is non-optional. SMB says the AM meeting exists to identify fresh news / stocks in play and to prepare specific ideas before the bell. Bear Bull Traders says serious day traders must identify the stock in play in premarket prep and then size more aggressively only when the A+ ORB appears. Live Traders says it typically goes into the open with five to ten stocks on the battlefield and then waits for the proper pattern. Warrior’s version is simply narrower and more momentum-biased: it wants the one or two most obvious names, usually low-float, usually exploding already, usually with retail crowd participation.

There is also a meaningful quantitative contrast. SMB’s public “stocks in play” process pays attention to gaps of ±3% with at least 10% of ADV and then grades catalysts, requiring a score of 8/10 or better to be tradable. Bear Bull Traders publicly emphasizes “stocks in play” with catalysts strong enough to support a clean ORB and points to a coauthored academic paper finding that 5-minute ORBs performed much better when restricted to stocks in play. Warrior’s public material is more aggressive: newer PDF criteria ask for RVOL ≥ 5x, already up ≥10%, and often seek under-$20, low-float setups where scarcity can produce parabolic moves.

The practical implication is important: if a trader wants the highest-volatility, highest-slippage, highest-discretion version of the premarket news strategy, Warrior’s public method is the closest fit. If the trader wants the same premarket-prep framework but with more liquidity, less spread risk, and more institutionally crowded names, the public practices of SMB and Bear Bull Traders pull in that direction. Live Traders sits somewhere in the middle, emphasizing morning gaps, a prepared watchlist, and thesis-invalidated exits, but not making low-float scarcity as central as Warrior does.

Three failure modes recur across Warrior’s own public examples. First, the stock was already too extended premarket, so the open offered little edge left to capture; BURG and some other recap comments fit this pattern. Second, liquidity disappeared or a hidden seller absorbed the breakout, which is exactly how Ross describes NSYS. Third, the open breakout failed so quickly that slippage exceeded the intended stop, which is the clearest lesson from FFHL and from other public warnings about light premarket liquidity and wide spreads.

A more nuanced reading of the public examples is this: the Warrior method does not just need a catalyst. It needs a catalyst that creates sustained crowd attention. Warrior’s own writing repeatedly ties good follow-through to the obvious stock — the one that is already leading percentage gainers, already printing volume, already attracting traders, and already mapping cleanly against premarket highs or other nearby levels. When the name is obvious, the edge appears to come from rapid crowd coordination around simple levels; when it is not, the same breakout logic becomes fragile very quickly.
Risks, legal, and practical limits

From a regulatory and broker-operations perspective, the biggest practical constraint is that premarket trading is structurally more dangerous than regular-session trading. FINRA warns that extended-hours trading is less liquid, often more volatile, and takes place in markets that are not linked the same way they are during regular hours, which means customers can get partial fills or inferior prices more easily. FINRA also notes that brokers may set different extended-hours windows, products, and order-type limitations, and that many firms will accept only limit orders in extended hours. Those points line up closely with Warrior’s own premarket page, which says premarket sessions are less liquid, wider-spread, electronically matched, and typically limit-order only.

Margin and account status matter as well. FINRA’s day-trading page says a U.S. trader who is coded as a pattern day trader generally needs to maintain $25,000 in a margin account and can trade only up to allowed day-trading buying power; the page also notes that day trading in a cash account is treated very differently. Warrior’s public content does not present one universal “required account size” for the strategy, but some public worksheets and examples assume margin access, frequent intraday trading, and the ability to scale share count by cents-to-stop. In other words: the strategy can be described without a broker/account assumption, but executing it the way Ross describes publicly almost certainly assumes infrastructure well beyond a casual brokerage app.

Warrior’s own disclosures are also explicit that the strategy is high-risk and that Ross’s results are not typical. Multiple official Warrior pages say most day traders lose money, and the site cites academic studies showing profitability is concentrated among a small minority of traders. That does not invalidate the strategy, but it does mean a reader should treat public recaps as teaching material, not as evidence that average retail traders can reproduce the results.

There are also meaningful data and execution limitations specific to Warrior’s style. Low-float names can halt repeatedly; spreads are wider, especially premarket; float data can be delayed or wrong for fresh IPOs or newly listed / reverse-split names; and the support docs explicitly say traders may occasionally need to check filings when float data is missing or suspect. Recent Warrior recaps add the operational point that direct routing speed matters, because latency turns fast scalps into worse fills. All of that makes the public Warrior method unusually sensitive to data quality, routing quality, and manual reaction speed.

From an ethical standpoint, the safest framing is simple: this is a strategy for reacting to publicly available market data and public news catalysts, not for blind-copying an educator’s orders. Warrior explicitly warns viewers not to mirror trade and repeatedly presents the material as educational. In practice, that means the only defensible use of these materials is to build and test a personal, rules-based process, ideally in simulation first, rather than to chase someone else’s prints in real time.
Actionable checklist and watchlist template

The concise checklist below is the public Warrior method stripped to operational essentials. It is best read as a preflight routine, not a guarantee of edge. It is synthesized from Warrior’s public video, written strategy pages, scanners docs, and stock-selection PDF.

Checklist

    Confirm your broker actually supports the extended-hours session you intend to trade, and confirm whether only limit orders are allowed.
    Pull the Top Gappers / Top Gainers list early and check whether the stock is showing the modern Warrior-style filters: high RVOL, already moving, obvious catalyst, preferred price band, and low float.
    Narrow the list to the top 2–3 obvious leaders only. If it is not obvious, it probably is not a Warrior-style A-setup.
    Reject names with no explainable catalyst, wide spreads, thin liquidity, or thick overhead resistance on the daily chart.
    On the chart, mark premarket high, any premarket flag, premarket pivot, nearby whole / half-dollar levels, and key daily levels.
    Decide the trigger before the bell: premarket flag break, premarket-high break, red-to-green, or 1-minute ORB.
    Predefine the invalidation level and dollar risk. Then set size by: shares = dollar risk ÷ cents-to-stop.
    If trading premarket, remember Warrior’s own warning: no automatic stop orders, so treat it as breakout or bailout and keep your sell key ready.
    Take an initial partial at the first planned target, then move the stop to breakeven and manage the remainder with red-candle / extension-bar logic.
    Stop pressing once the market is cold, the obvious stock has faded, or your own daily risk parameters are hit.

Open questions and limitations

Some parts of the public Warrior curriculum remain underspecified unless you buy the membership or watch all the source videos directly. The public course preview makes clear that the paid course goes deeper on entries such as Break of Pre-Market Highs, Pre-Market Pivot, 1-minute / 5-minute ORB, and Pre-Market Gap & Go trades, but the public preview does not disclose the full rule detail of each lesson. Likewise, among the retrieved YouTube items, only How to Day Trade Pre-Market exposed a full public chapter list with timestamps; other official retrieved videos were identifiable, but not timestamped in the search snippet.

The empirical section also has unavoidable blind spots. Several public trade recaps describe entries, adds, and overall P/L without a full execution ladder, which means some gross-move estimates necessarily use average-entry approximations or midpoint exits. That is good enough for analyzing the shape of the strategy, but not good enough to infer a verified edge.

Finally, a few important infrastructure assumptions are not standardized in public Warrior materials and should therefore be treated as unspecified in any strict reconstruction: exact broker and routing stack, exact market-data package, exact share locates / short inventory assumptions, exact latency assumptions, exact rules for when Ross allows premarket size vs open size, and whether a particular implementation is being run from a large account, small account, or simulator. One public worksheet does show a small-account variant with 7:00 a.m.–11:00 a.m. ET, under 20M float in hot markets / under 10M in cold markets, and Top 5 on gap scanner + positive catalyst as premarket requirements — but that reads as an illustrative plan template, not a canonical rule-set.
